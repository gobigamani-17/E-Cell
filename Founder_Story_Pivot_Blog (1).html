The pivot that
saved Zomato

Deepinder Goyal and Pankaj Chaddah didn't set out to build a food-delivery giant. They just wanted to stop queuing up for paper menus.

In 2008, Deepinder Goyal and Pankaj Chaddah were consultants at Bain & Company in Delhi, watching colleagues waste time queuing up just to read paper restaurant menus in the office pantry. That daily annoyance, not a grand vision, is where the company that became Zomato actually started.

📋 A side project for two people

Goyal and Chaddah scanned the restaurant menus their office relied on and uploaded them to an internal site — purely to solve their own problem. There was no business plan yet, just two colleagues tired of a small daily friction. Both kept their full-time jobs at Bain while building it on the side.

🚀 From internal tool to public launch

The internal version caught on fast inside the office. Seeing the demand, they launched it publicly in July 2008 as Foodiebay, a restaurant-menu directory for Delhi-NCR — manually collecting, scanning, and uploading menus from thousands of restaurants. By late 2008, it was already the largest restaurant directory in the city.

🔄 The rename that marked the real pivot

In 2010, after Info Edge invested in the company, Foodiebay was renamed Zomato — a signal that the ambition had grown far beyond a menu directory. From there, the business kept reinventing itself: from listings and reviews into food delivery, then cloud kitchens, then quick commerce, rebuilding the model again each time the market shifted underneath it.

The takeaway: the idea didn't change all that much — access to food information, made easier. What changed was the willingness to keep rebuilding the business around it, again and again, as the opportunity got bigger than the original problem.
